# Nepal Drone Law & Regulatory Research: Comprehensive Knowledge Base

**Prepared as a foundation for an interactive drone flight-planning application**
**Research date: June 30, 2026 | Sources: CAAN, Sagarmatha National Park, government-hosted PDFs, gazetted Acts, and corroborating secondary sources (see Bibliography, §15)**

---

## How to read this document

This is Phase 1 of what is realistically a multi-phase research program. It is built from roughly 20 targeted searches and direct fetches against CAAN's own published rulebook, the Sagarmatha National Park office site, Nepal's Privacy Act text, and a wide cross-section of legal and tourism-industry sources that quote or summarize Nepal's drone procedures. It is **not** a substitute for a lawyer's sign-off or a direct confirmation call to CAAN/MOHA before a real flight — Nepali aviation procedure is administered in person, in Kathmandu, by people who can and do exercise discretion.

**Confidence ratings** are applied to every substantive claim:

- **High** — drawn directly from a primary legal/government source (the CAAN UASR text itself, the CAAN drone webpage, the Sagarmatha National Park office page, a gazetted Act, FAA/EASA/CAAC/BCAA primary sources for the comparison section), or corroborated identically by three or more independent secondary sources.
- **Medium** — stated consistently by two or more independent secondary sources (typically Kathmandu-based trekking/legal-services sites) but not directly verified against an unambiguous primary-source clause, OR drawn from a single primary-adjacent source (e.g., an official CAAN circular PDF) without broader corroboration.
- **Low** — appears in only one source, or sources actively conflict with each other, or the figure looks like marketing copy rather than a cited tariff/penalty schedule. Treat Low-confidence figures (most fee and fine amounts below) as illustrative ranges to verify directly with CAAN/MOHA, not as quotable numbers for the app's UI.

A dedicated section (§16) collects every place official guidance is ambiguous, contradictory, or simply silent, with a recommended interpretation for each.

**A structural note on Nepal's drone law, up front:** unlike the FAA or EASA, Nepal does not regulate drones through one consolidated rulebook. Three documents do almost all of the work, and the app's rules engine should be built around this three-layer structure rather than a single "the law says" source of truth:

1. **CAAN's Unmanned Aircraft System Requirements (UASR)** — the technical/operational rulebook (altitude, VLOS, categories, licensing).
2. **Ministry of Home Affairs' RPA/Drone (UAV) Working Procedure, 2075** — the security-clearance and permit-routing procedure (who signs off, in what order).
3. A shifting set of **site-specific procedures** issued by individual custodians of sensitive places (Sagarmatha National Park's own drone procedure is the clearest documented example) that layer additional, non-CAAN approvals on top of the first two.

Almost every public confusion this research surfaced (§16) comes from secondary sources blending these three layers, or quoting an older superseded version of one of them, as if there were a single number that applies everywhere.

## Executive Summary

Civilian drone ("RPA/Drone" or "UAV" in Nepali official usage) operation in Nepal is legal but tightly gated. Every drone, regardless of weight, must be **registered** with the Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal (CAAN) and obtain a separate **flight permit** before each operation; registration alone never authorizes flight (High confidence — this is stated explicitly on the registration certificate itself). The operative technical rulebook is CAAN's **Unmanned Aircraft System Requirements (UASR), Issue 01, dated April 2021**, issued under Rule 82 of the Civil Aviation Regulation, 2058 (2002), which sets a 100 m (328 ft) AGL altitude ceiling, a 300 m horizontal range from the pilot, mandatory visual line-of-sight, a hard 25 kg gross-mass cap, and a four-tier weight classification (High confidence, primary source).

Layered on top of CAAN's technical rules is the Ministry of Home Affairs' **RPA/Drone Working Procedure, 2075 (2018/19)**, which routes every flight permit through a security-clearance process — District Administration Offices for lighter/lower-altitude flights, MOHA directly for everything else — and imposes a roughly 1 km no-fly buffer around security installations and VIP residences (Medium-High confidence, corroborated across many independent sources but not independently re-verified against the Working Procedure's own text, which is not posted in full on any official site this research could locate).

A third layer applies in nationally protected areas: Sagarmatha (Everest) National Park publishes its **own** drone procedure requiring CAAN registration, a separate Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation approval, and a District Administration Office (Solukhumbu) approval — confirmed directly from the park's own government website (High confidence). The same pattern applies, with less direct documentation, to the Annapurna Conservation Area and other protected areas.

Foreign nationals face no separate "pilot exam" requirement under the rules as published, but in practice clear more approval layers than residents (Department of Tourism, MOHA security clearance, and — for trekking regions — the relevant conservation-area authority), and tourism-industry sources report total foreign-visitor costs and timelines that vary enormously (anywhere from roughly USD 50 to several hundred dollars, days to months), which is exactly the kind of figure this report flags as Low confidence pending direct confirmation.

Enforcement is real but not run on a published fixed-penalty schedule: violations are prosecuted under the **Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal Act, 2053 (1996)** and the **Electronic Transactions Act, 2063 (2008)**, with on-the-spot seizure, police referral, and CAAN auction of confiscated drones as the consistently documented enforcement mechanism (Medium-High confidence); specific fine amounts quoted by tourism blogs range from NPR 2,000 to NPR 500,000+ depending on the source and have not been traced to a single authoritative tariff (Low confidence — flagged explicitly in §16).

Nepal has no GIS-published no-fly-zone shapefile or polygon dataset equivalent to the FAA's UAS Facility Maps or EASA member-state geozone portals (this absence was specifically checked for and not found — see §7). Any "no-fly zone map" layer the planned app builds will necessarily be assembled from named places (airports, parks, heritage sites) with general-vicinity coordinates rather than from an official authoritative polygon file, and this report is explicit throughout about which coordinates are well-established public landmarks (airports, UNESCO sites) versus which restriction categories (specific military installations, VIP residences) this report deliberately does not attempt to pin to precise coordinates, because no authoritative source publishes them and inventing precision would make the app's compliance data *less* trustworthy, not more.

## 1. Legal Framework

### 1.1 The instrument hierarchy

Nepali drone law sits inside the general civil aviation legal structure, not as a standalone statute. In descending order of legal authority:

1. **Constitution of Nepal, 2072 (2015)**, Article 28 — guarantees privacy of body, residence, property, documents/data, correspondence, and character as a fundamental right; the legal backstop for every privacy-related drone restriction (High confidence, primary constitutional text corroborated by multiple legal sources).
2. **Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal Act, 2053 (1996)** — the founding statute that creates CAAN and gives it regulatory and penal authority over all aircraft, including unmanned ones; Section 25 is the general penalty provision invoked in virtually every drone-violation prosecution found in this research (High confidence, primary source fetched directly).
3. **Civil Aviation Regulation, 2058 (2002)** — the regulation made under the 1996 Act; **Rule 82** is the specific provision CAAN cites as its authority to issue UAS-specific requirements (High confidence, stated explicitly on the cover of the UASR itself).
4. **Good Governance (Management and Operation) Act, 2064 (2007/08)**, Section 45 — the enabling provision the Ministry of Home Affairs relied on to issue its own drone working procedure, separate from CAAN's technical rules (Medium confidence — stated by one detailed secondary legal-explainer source, structurally plausible and consistent with how MOHA's procedure operates, but not independently confirmed against the Act's text).
5. **Civil Aviation Security Rule, 2073 (2016)**, first amended 2076 (2019) — general aviation-security regulation; CAAN's own documents page lists this alongside the drone-specific materials, suggesting it forms part of the security-clearance backdrop for drone permits (Medium confidence — listed on CAAN's official documents page but its drone-specific content was not independently reviewed in full).
6. **Electronic Transactions Act, 2063 (2008)** — invoked alongside the CAAA Act 2053 in drone-violation prosecutions, presumably covering the digital/recording-device dimension of unauthorized drone use (High confidence on the citation pattern, corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources).

## 2. Drone Classification

CAAN's UASR (Reg. 101.5(b)) classifies every drone by Maximum Take-Off Weight (MTOW), including everything attached to or carried by the aircraft. This is the canonical classification — confirmed by directly reading the regulation text — and every secondary source found agrees with it, though several round the boundaries differently in casual writing.

| Category | Designation | MTOW range | Registration required? | Typical permit pathway | Operational notes | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Very Low Risk | Under 250 g | **Yes — no weight-based exemption appears in the regulation text itself.** CAAN's own drone webpage carves out only "small flying toys," not camera-equipped Category A consumer drones, for private-premises-only use without permission. | District Administration Office (for flight above ~200 ft / outside private property) | Most secondary sources warn that tourists routinely assume sub-250 g drones (DJI Mini-class) are exempt the way they are in the US/EU/UK — this is incorrect for Nepal. | High (registration requirement) / Medium (the 200 ft / private-property carve-out specifics) |
| B | Low Risk | 250 g – 2 kg | Yes | District Administration Office for operations above 200 ft AGL | Below 200 ft on private property, multiple sources describe an informal carve-out from needing a CAAN flight permission (not from registration). | Medium |
| C | Regulated Low Risk | 2 kg – 25 kg | Yes, plus physical verification of the drone at CAAN's office | Ministry of Home Affairs (not DAO) | Third-party liability insurance reported as required for this category. | Medium |
| D | Regulated High Risk | Above 25 kg | Yes — but note Reg. 101.37 of the UASR caps the *entire Part 101 framework* at 25 kg gross mass, meaning drones over 25 kg may fall outside what UASR governs and require bespoke CAAN authorization rather than a standard Category D permit pathway. | Ministry of Home Affairs / bespoke CAAN authorization | Used in tourism-industry sources mainly as a label for heavy-lift cargo, industrial, and inspection platforms (e.g., the DJI FlyCart 30 used on Everest, see §12). | Medium (the label exists and is used) / **this report flags the >25kg/Part 101 interaction as a genuine ambiguity — see §16.** |

No separate insurance, pilot-certification, or operational-limitation table is published per category beyond what is captured above; CAAN's UASR Part 102 (remote pilot licensing) applies in principle across categories rather than scaling cleanly by category, which is itself one of the structural gaps noted in §16.

## 3. Registration Requirements

**Which drones require registration:** All of them. UASR Reg. 101.5(a) states "every person lawfully entitled to the possession of a UA who will operate a UA in Nepal shall register that UA" — no weight floor is written into this clause (High confidence, primary source). CAAN's own drone webpage reinforces this with only a narrow private-property "small flying toy" carve-out.

**Where:** Flight Safety Standards Department (FSSD), CAAN, Sinamangal, Kathmandu — confirmed as the registration office across every source, including CAAN's own application form (High confidence). Online registration (caanepal.gov.np/drone) reportedly launched in 2025, alongside the long-standing in-person process; multiple sources from late 2025/early 2026 describe the online portal as unreliable, so in-person FSSD registration remains the dependable path (Medium confidence).

**Required documents** (per UASR Appendix I, the primary-source registration requirements, supplemented by consistent secondary-source detail):

- Completed application **Form RPA/Form/001**
- One recent passport-size colour photograph of the applicant
- Drone details: manufacturer, model, serial number, manufacture date, type, payload type, take-off weight, colour, retailer name
- Copy of citizenship certificate, valid passport, or company registration certificate (as applicable)
- Copy of the drone's manual/specification sheet
- Colour photo of the drone clearly showing its model and serial number
- Stated purpose of operation
- **Either** a copy of the retailer's VAT bill (if purchased in Nepal) **or** a copy of the customs clearance certificate (if imported)
- For foreign nationals: passport with valid Nepal visa, and (per several independent sources) a Tribhuvan International Airport customs declaration of the drone on arrival
- CE marking or a type-approval certificate for the drone model (Medium confidence — appears in multiple 2026-dated tourism-industry sources, not found in the UASR text itself)

**Costs:** This is the single most inconsistent figure across all sources gathered, and the app's UI should not hard-code a number without independent confirmation. Two independent late-2025 sources converge on **USD 50 + 13% VAT for foreign nationals**, with one explicitly stating CAAN raised the fee from roughly USD 10 to USD 50 in April 2025 and added a USD 10 renewal fee. Other sources (2025–2026, both tourism-blog in nature) instead cite NPR 10,000–15,000 or a flat USD 150 for foreign nationals. **Confidence: Low** on any specific number — the direction (foreigners pay materially more than the Nepali-citizen rate, and registration fees rose at some point around 2025) is more reliable than the exact figure.

**Validity & renewal:** Registration certificates are reported as valid for **one year**, renewable (Medium-High confidence, consistent across most sources, though not independently confirmed against UASR's own text, which references renewal without stating a fixed term in the portions retrieved).

**Processing time:** Two to three business days after a complete application is accepted (Medium confidence, single detailed source, plausible and specific).

**Exemptions:** None found beyond the narrow "small flying toy operated entirely within private premises" carve-out described on CAAN's own webpage — and even that is described there as a *flight-permission* carve-out, not unambiguously a *registration* carve-out, which is itself flagged in §16.

**Registration ≠ permission to fly.** This is stated explicitly on the certificate of registration itself (UASR Appendix III, Note 8, primary source, High confidence): a separate flight permit is always required.

## 4. Permit System

Nepal does not operate a single unified "drone permit"; it operates a **routing procedure** that determines which office issues the flight authorization, based on category, altitude, location, and operator nationality/purpose. The clearest documented version of the routing logic:

### 4.1 General routing logic (Medium-High confidence — consistent across many independent sources, structurally corroborated by the official CAAN drone webpage's "tourists must obtain prior permission from following agencies" framing, though the *exact* threshold numbers vary slightly between sources)

1. **Category A/B (under 2 kg), flown below roughly 200 ft AGL, on private property** → no separate flight permission reported as necessary beyond registration (though local police/security-agency courtesy notice is still recommended for any public-area flight).
2. **Category A/B, flown above ~200 ft AGL or in a public area** → flight permit application to the relevant **District Administration Office (DAO)**, which coordinates with the local civil aviation office if one exists.
3. **Category C/D, or any commercial operation, or flight in a border/sensitive area** → application routed to the **Ministry of Home Affairs** directly, which CAAN itself is described as forwarding the security-clearance request to (adding roughly 5–15 working days per one detailed source).
4. **National parks / conservation areas** → an additional, independent approval from the **Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation** (or the relevant conservation area authority, e.g., ACAP for Annapurna, MCAP for Manaslu) plus the local DAO, layered on top of 1–3 above. Confirmed directly for Sagarmatha National Park from the park's own website (§1.2, row 6).
5. **Heritage/religious sites** → coordination with the Department of Archaeology or the relevant temple trust board (Medium confidence, single detailed source, but structurally consistent with the general "do not disturb heritage sites" rule appearing in CAAN's own published conditions).
6. **Foreign nationals**, regardless of category, additionally clear the **Department of Tourism** and submit an authorization letter to a local person/agency, biodata, citizenship/passport copy, and a compliance-commitment letter (Medium-High confidence, the foreign-national document list is the most consistently repeated checklist across sources).
7. **Frequency/spectrum**: if the drone's radio link exceeds roughly 2.4 MHz of bandwidth, a separate clearance from the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology (successor to the old Ministry of Information and Communication) is reportedly required (Medium confidence — this requirement traces back to the historical 2015 Directive and is repeated by some but not all current sources, so it may or may not still be actively enforced as a separate step).

### 4.2 Permit characteristics

| Attribute | Detail | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | Maximum 3 months from issue (UASR Reg. 101.9(a)(1) caps area-approvals at 3 months; MOHA permit validity is separately and consistently reported as 3 months across many secondary sources) | High |
| Renewal | Permitted with a stated reason; renewal period itself capped at a further 3 months | Medium-High |
| Transferability | Not transferable to another person or organization | Medium |
| Required underlying documents | Registration certificate/UIN, flight-consent letter, purpose and exact location (often requested as a Google Maps/flight-location map), start/end dates and times, foreign-national authorization letter where applicable | Medium-High |
| Insurance | Third-party liability insurance reported as required for Category C/D drones | Medium |
| Appeals | UASR Part 102 provides an explicit appeal mechanism for remote-pilot-licence decisions (denial, suspension, revocation, or new conditions) — apply in writing to CAAN; no equivalent appeal text was located specifically for flight-permit (as opposed to pilot-licence) denials. | High (licence appeals) / Low (permit-denial appeals — likely follows general administrative-law channels, not drone-specific) |
| Disaster/emergency flights | A special expedited pathway exists for domestic or foreign experts to fly under CAAN/government approval during a declared disaster or crisis, but the drone must operate under the direct authority of a security/liaison officer (in some sources specifically a Nepal Army officer) in the control area. | Medium-High (consistent historical and current sourcing) |
| Prohibited payloads | Explosives, biological agents, anything posing a security risk, animals, and humans may not be carried by an RPA/drone. | Medium-High |

### 4.3 Use-case notes

- **Recreational/hobbyist:** Same registration + permit chain as everyone else; no separate "recreational" carve-out beyond the private-property/sub-200ft scenario above.
- **Commercial (photography, videography, filming):** Additionally requires a letter of recommendation from the relevant ministry; one source specifically notes a 25% surcharge on the base permit cost for documentary/cinematic filming (Low confidence, single source).
- **Survey/mapping/agriculture/infrastructure inspection:** Routed as Category C/D commercial operations in practice; no separate published permit class was found — treat as "commercial, MOHA-routed" until confirmed otherwise.
- **Government operations:** Security agencies flying RPA/drones are described as needing to coordinate with relevant ministries and CAAN, suggesting an internal rather than public-facing permit process.
- **NGO operations:** No distinct published category; in practice follows the foreign-organization or commercial pathway depending on the NGO's registration status and the operator's nationality.
- **Research:** A distinct documented pathway exists requiring Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation permission (for protected-area research) layered on the standard chain (per CAAN's own drone webpage, which lists "research and study purpose" as a named category).

## 5. Flight Rules

This section is anchored in the UASR primary text wherever possible (High confidence items are direct regulation citations), supplemented by the MOHA-layer rules where the UASR is silent.

| Rule | Requirement | Source / confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum altitude** | 100 m (328 ft) AGL under standard operating conditions (Reg. 101.7, 101.25); operating above this requires CAAN/ATS approval and, beyond 5 km from an aerodrome, a NOTAM filed at least 24 hours ahead. | High (primary source) |
| **Minimum altitude** | None specified in the regulation. | High (confirmed absence) |
| **Visual Line of Sight (VLOS)** | Mandatory for standard operations (Reg. 101.27); defined as direct, unaided visual contact (corrective lenses/contacts permitted; electronic/optical aids are not, except via a trained observer in an FPV setup). | High (primary source) |
| **Horizontal range from pilot** | 300 m under standard conditions (Reg. 101.7(a)(2)). *Many tourism-industry sources still quote 500 m — this is the figure from the superseded 2015 Directive; treat 300 m as current.* | High (primary source) / flagged conflict, see §16 |
| **FPV operations** | Permitted only with a trained, competent UA observer who independently maintains VLOS and surrounding-airspace awareness and stays in direct communication with the pilot (Reg. 101.27(d)(2)). | High (primary source) |
| **Night operations** | UASR Reg. 101.29(a)(2) prohibits flying at night outright, and Reg. 101.31 ("Night Operations"), which would presumably set out an authorization pathway, is marked **RESERVED** — i.e., not yet written. | **BVLOS operations** | Not available as a standard pathway. | **Flights over people** | Prohibited unless the person is directly participating in the operation, sheltered under a structure/inside a stationary vehicle, or — with consent — no closer than 15 m; absent consent, a 30 m horizontal buffer from any uninvolved person is required (Reg. 101.35). | High (primary source) |
| **Flights over roads/vehicles** | No drone may be operated from a moving vehicle, vessel, or manned aircraft (Reg. 101.43(b)); flying *over* roads/traffic is governed by the general over-people and populated-area restrictions rather than a distinct road-specific rule. | High (primary source) |
| **Flights over buildings/urban operations** | Standard conditions bar operating "over a populated area" outright (Reg. 101.7(b)(3)) — this is a blanket prohibition without a stated population-density threshold, meaning routine urban flight in Kathmandu, Pokhara, etc. functionally requires a CAAN-approved area rather than falling under standard conditions at all. | High (primary source) |
| **Rural operations** | No distinct rural carve-out; the same standard-conditions envelope applies, simply with fewer of the populated-area/people-proximity triggers in practice. | High (by absence) |
| **Weather restrictions** | VMC (Visual Meteorological Conditions) only; no flight in or into cloud; pilot must be able to maintain VLOS through the whole flight (Reg. 101.27(c)(3), 101.29). | High (primary source) |
| **Airport/aerodrome distance** | 5 km from the movement area of any aerodrome without specific authorization (Reg. 101.7(b)(5), 101.23); even with authorization at an uncontrolled aerodrome, flight is capped at 100 m AGL, 15 minutes' endurance, 300 m range, and requires a UA observer. Controlled aerodromes require ATC authorization. | High (primary source) |
| **Military/security-installation distance** | A roughly 1,000 m (1 km) buffer around "security agencies and sensitive areas" is consistently reported across both the historical 2015 Directive and current MOHA-procedure-derived secondary sources; one detailed source describes a tiered system (1 km for military/"safety office," 500 m for "other safety departments"), which has not been independently confirmed against an official text. | Medium-High (1 km figure) / Low (the tiered 500m/1km split) |
| **Government building restrictions** | The President's, Vice-President's, and Prime Minister's offices/residences (Singha Durbar and related VVIP locations in Kathmandu) carry a reported ~1 km aerial-radius restriction, corroborated by two independent sources. | Medium-High |
| **National park restrictions** | Confirmed directly (Sagarmatha National Park) as requiring CAAN registration plus DNPWC approval plus local DAO approval before the park issues its own permission; the same general pattern (additional conservation-area-specific layer) is reported, with less direct documentation, for Annapurna Conservation Area, Langtang, and Manaslu. | High (Sagarmatha, primary) / Medium (other parks, by reported pattern) |
| **Wildlife restrictions** | No drone-specific wildlife-disturbance rule was located beyond the general national-park/conservation-area permission requirement above; Sagarmatha National Park's own reasoning for restricting *helicopter* sightseeing flights (noise disturbance to Himalayan tahr/goral, breeding/feeding disruption) is well documented and is the most likely policy rationale extended to drones in the same areas. | Medium |
| **Border restrictions** | A 5 km horizontal buffer from international boundaries is reported across multiple sources structurally similar to the airport buffer, though this was not located inside the UASR text itself (the UASR's 5 km clause is specifically aerodrome-worded) — likely a MOHA-procedure-layer rule rather than a CAAN/UASR one. | Medium |
| **Emergency-response restrictions** | A drone may not be operated over an area where fire, police, or other public-safety/emergency operations are underway, without the approval of the person in charge of that operation (Reg. 101.7(c)). | High (primary source) |
| **Critical infrastructure (hydropower, power stations, transmission, communications towers)** | Generally captured under the broader "restricted/prohibited area as defined by Government of Nepal" clause (Reg. 101.7(b)(1)-(2)) rather than a named, drone-specific infrastructure rule; no published list of which hydropower or transmission sites are formally gazetted as restricted/prohibited areas was located. | Low — **this is a genuine documentation gap, not just a sourcing gap; flagged in §16.** |
| **Crowd restrictions** | Flying over crowds/public gatherings/sporting events is consistently described as prohibited without specific authorization. | Medium-High |
| **Privacy/photography/video restrictions** | Layered: UASR's own historical predecessor explicitly banned privacy-infringing filming from "toy" drones; the Individual Privacy Act 2075 and National Penal Code 2074 supply the general criminal/civil privacy exposure for any aerial photography of identifiable people without consent (see §1.2, rows 9–10). | Medium-High |
| **Delivery operations** | No standard published pathway; CAAN's general framework (registration + permit + category rules) would apply, but Nepal's most prominent real-world example — Airlift Technology's cargo-drone resupply operation on Everest — was reportedly **cancelled by the government in 2026** after operating since 2024 in partnership with the Sagarmatha Pollution Control Committee, illustrating that even an apparently successful, safety-positive delivery use case can be reversed at the government's discretion. (See §12, Historical Timeline.) | Medium (the cancellation; secondary journalism source, not yet corroborated by an official government statement in this research pass) |
| **Autonomous flight** | UASR's definition of "fly-away" implies awareness of autonomous/pre-programmed flight as a distinct risk category; multiple tourism-industry sources state fully autonomous/pre-programmed flight is prohibited and that radio-transmission-based direct control is the standard required method, consistent with ICAO guidance. | Medium |
| **Swarm operations** | No Nepal-specific swarm rule was located in any source. | Low — gap, not just low confidence; treat as unaddressed. |
| **Signal interference / radio frequency** | The operator's control-link frequency must not interfere with security-agency communications, and (per some sources) a separate Ministry of Communication and Information Technology clearance is required above ~2.4 MHz bandwidth. | Medium |
| **Right of way** | UA must give way to and remain clear of all manned aircraft, on the ground and in flight, at all times (Reg. 101.33). | High (primary source) |
| **Single-operator, single-aircraft rule** | A person may operate only one UA at a time (Reg. 101.7(d), 102.5(d)). | High (primary source) |
| **Alcohol/drugs** | No remote pilot, flight crew member, or UA observer may operate within 8 hours of consuming alcohol, while under its influence, or while impaired by any drug (Reg. 101.45). | High (primary source) |
| **Accident/incident reporting** | Any UA operation causing injury to a person or damage to property other than the UA itself must be reported to CAAN within 48 hours (Reg. 101.009). | High (primary source) |

## 6. Restricted Airspace

**A methodological note before the table:** Nepal does not publish an official, coordinate-level no-fly-zone dataset. Every coordinate below falls into one of two honest categories, and the table says explicitly which applies to each row:

- **(A) Well-established public landmark coordinates** — airports (published in global aeronautical databases under their ICAO/IATA codes) and UNESCO World Heritage Site locations (published by UNESCO and every mapping service). These are safe to use as map anchors; what's *not* officially published is the exact restriction radius/polygon around them, which this report gives as the most-corroborated reported figure, clearly marked as reported-not-gazetted.
- **(B) Categories this report deliberately does not pin to specific coordinates** — individual military installations and named VIP residences. Two reasons: no official source publishes a coordinate list for these, and a from-scratch, original compilation of "where Nepal's sensitive security sites are" is not something this report should manufacture by guesswork even for a protective/compliance purpose. For these categories, the app should treat the restriction as a *rule* (e.g., "1 km buffer around any security installation") to be checked against a general-purpose, independently-sourced basemap layer (see §7) rather than a list this research invents.

## 7. Geographic Data Collection — Recommendations for the App's GIS Layer

Because no official Nepal-government no-fly-zone shapefile or polygon dataset was located in this research (this was specifically searched for and not found — treat that absence itself as a Medium-confidence finding, since a negative result is harder to be fully certain about than a positive one), the flight-planning app's map layer will need to be assembled from several *separate* authoritative sources rather than one official download. Recommended building blocks:

- **Administrative boundaries (district, municipality):** Survey Department of Nepal (the national mapping authority) and the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX), which hosts Nepal administrative-boundary shapefiles contributed by OCHA/UN agencies — generally reliable and free to use.
- **Airports/helipads:** OurAirports and OpenFlights both maintain free, regularly updated global airport datasets with ICAO-coded coordinates that will cover every CAAN-listed Nepali aerodrome; cross-reference against CAAN's own airport-profile pages for the handful of domestic strips.
- **Protected areas / national parks / conservation areas:** The World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA, run by UNEP-WCMC/IUCN) publishes polygon boundaries for Sagarmatha, Annapurna, Langtang, Manaslu, and Nepal's other gazetted protected areas — this is the right source for park *boundaries*, even though it won't tell you the drone-permit rule layered on top (that has to come from this report's §6 and direct confirmation with DNPWC).
- **UNESCO World Heritage Sites:** UNESCO's own World Heritage Centre publishes precise coordinates and boundary/buffer-zone polygons for the Kathmandu Valley sites.
- **Elevation:** SRTM (NASA) or Copernicus DEM — both freely licensed, both adequate resolution for flight-planning altitude-above-terrain calculations in Nepal's mountainous terrain (important given CAAN's rules are AGL-based, and Nepal's terrain relief makes AGL very different from a flat-earth altitude assumption).
- **Roads / urban boundaries / population density:** OpenStreetMap (community-maintained but strong coverage in Nepal, including in trekking regions) for roads and built-up areas; WorldPop or the Nepal census (Central Bureau of Statistics) for population-density layers relevant to the "populated area" flight restriction.
- **Military / sensitive-site polygons:** Deliberately not recommended as a "go find a dataset" item — see §6's methodological note. If the app needs this layer, the right move is a direct written request to CAAN/MOHA for an official list, not assembling one from open sources, given the sensitivity.

**Coordinate reference system:** WGS84 (EPSG:4326) is the de facto standard across every source above and is what CAAN's own aeronautical charts would use; recommended as the app's canonical CRS, projecting to a local UTM zone (Nepal spans UTM zones 44N and 45N) only for distance/buffer calculations.

**Licensing:** OSM is ODbL (share-alike attribution); WDPA and UNESCO data are generally free for non-commercial/research use with attribution but check current terms before commercial deployment; HDX-hosted boundary files vary by contributor — check each dataset's individual license page.

## 10. Enforcement

**Legal basis:** Violations are prosecuted under the **Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal Act, 2053**, Section 25, and the **Electronic Transactions Act, 2063** (High confidence — this citation pairing is the most consistent enforcement-related fact across every source, including an official CAAN circular that states violators are "subject to punishment pursuant to Civil Aviation Authority Act (2053)").

**Documented enforcement mechanism** (Medium-High confidence, consistent structural description across many independent sources):

1. An unlicensed or non-compliant flight is identified (by police, army, CAAN inspectors, or — per several 2026-dated sources — increasingly by ordinary residents reporting suspicious activity).
2. The drone is seized immediately; seizure is explicitly **not** contingent on a prior warning.
3. A police report is generated and the drone, with the report, is sent to CAAN.
4. CAAN may auction the confiscated drone under prevailing law, with proceeds directed to a government revenue account.
5. Separately, a privacy-violation victim may file a civil/criminal complaint under the Individual Privacy Act (District Court, 3-month limitation period) — and notably, "unauthorized drone use"/"drone surveillance" is specifically named in legal commentary as one of the offense types that can additionally be pursued as a **state** party complaint, not only a private one.
6. CAAN retains inspection, testing, and records-demand authority over the UAS, the remote pilot, and any UA observer (UASR Reg. 101.007, primary source).
7. For remote-pilot-licence holders specifically, UASR Part 102 sets out a formal show-cause → response → cancellation/suspension process, including an explicit 90-day cap on a suspension if CAAN hasn't resolved the underlying case (Reg. 102.9–102.11, primary source, High confidence).

**Penalties — fines:** This is the weakest-sourced numeric claim in the entire report. Across roughly a dozen tourism/legal-blog sources gathered, first-offense "no permit" fines were variously quoted as NPR 2,000–5,000, NPR 5,000–50,000, NPR 25,000–100,000, and NPR 100,000–500,000+ for aggravated cases (airports/security zones) — **none of these were traced back to a single cited, dated, official tariff schedule or gazette notice.** The pattern (escalating fines with severity; airports/security/heritage-site violations treated as more serious; repeat-offense premiums) is plausible and broadly consistent with how the underlying CAAA Act's penalty clause typically operates, but the *specific numbers* should be treated as Low confidence, illustrative-only, and explicitly not surfaced as authoritative figures in the app's UI without direct confirmation from CAAN.

**Penalties — non-monetary:** Drone confiscation (auctioned, not returned) is the most consistently documented and highest-confidence penalty. Several 2026-dated sources also describe park-permit blacklisting for foreigners caught operating illegally in national parks, and the possibility of deportation for serious/repeat violations — both Medium confidence, plausible but not traced to a specific statutory provision.

**Privacy Act-specific penalty:** Up to 3 years' imprisonment and/or a fine up to NPR 30,000, plus court-ordered compensation (Medium-High confidence — this figure, unlike the general drone fines above, is repeated consistently and traces to the Privacy Act's own penalty clause as summarized by multiple independent legal sources).

**Case examples / public enforcement actions:** No specific, named, dated drone-confiscation case was located and independently verified in this research pass (the closest documented regulatory enforcement dispute found was the December 2024–2025 CAAN-vs-Sagarmatha National Park clash over *helicopter* sightseeing flights, not a drone case specifically — see §12 and §16 for why that dispute is still directly relevant to how drone jurisdiction works). This is a genuine gap; a dedicated pass through Nepali news archives (Kathmandu Post, Setopati, myRepublica, Onlinekhabar) for named drone-seizure stories would strengthen this section considerably.
